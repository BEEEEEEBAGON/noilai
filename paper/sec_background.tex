\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{The Vietnamese syllable on the page.}
A written Vietnamese syllable is onset + rime + tone, the rime being an optional glide, a nucleus and an optional coda \citep{thompson-1965-vietnamese,doan-1977-nguam,nguyen-1997-vietnamese}. Our parser recognizes 24 onsets (including the empty onset), 18 nuclei (eleven monophthongs, six diphthongs and the loan nucleus \vi{oo}), eleven codas (eight consonants, the two semivowels written \vi{i/y} and \vi{o/u}, and none) and six tones: \vi{ngang} (unmarked), \vi{huyền}, \vi{sắc}, \vi{hỏi}, \vi{ngã} and \vi{nặng} \citep{kirby-2011-vietnamese,pham-2003-vietnamese-tone}. The mapping from structure to letters is not one-to-one. The consonant /k/ is written \vi{q} before the glide, \vi{k} before \vi{e ê i} and \vi{c} elsewhere; the velar fricative and nasal are \vi{gh}/\vi{ngh} before those same front vowels and \vi{g}/\vi{ng} otherwise; the onset \vi{gi} absorbs a following \vi{i} (\vi{gì}, \vi{giêng}); the glide is written \vi{u} or \vi{o} depending on the nucleus; the nucleus \vi{i} is written \vi{y} after the glide and in the bare syllable; the short vowel \vi{ă} is written \vi{a} before the semivowel codas (\vi{tay} vs.\ \vi{tai}); and syllables that end in a stop take only the tones \vi{sắc} and \vi{nặng}. Two conventions place the tone mark. Both put it on the only vowel letter, on the last vowel letter before a consonant coda and on the middle of three vowel letters; they differ only on the two-letter open rimes \vi{oa}, \vi{oe} and \vi{uy} after an onset other than \vi{qu}, where the older convention marks the first letter (\vi{hòa}, \vi{khỏe}, \vi{thủy}) and the newer one the second (\vi{hoà}, \vi{khoẻ}, \vi{thuỷ}). Exactly 69 of the 6,611 syllables of the standard spelling list differ \citep{hunspell-vi}, both conventions are in everyday use, and the older one is the majority in running text (the press, Vietnamese Wikipedia, the default setting of the most common input method) while the newer one is the textbook convention (\placeholder{corpus count before Gate~1; DESIGN\_DECISIONS 6.3}). Appendix~\ref{app:rules} tabulates every rule as the engine implements it.

\paragraph{Three encodings of the same letters.}
Vietnamese letters carry up to two diacritics, a quality mark (\vi{ă â ê ô ơ ư}) and a tone mark. Unicode provides a precomposed code point for every combination, so the same text can be stored in NFC (one code point per letter) or in NFD (base letter plus combining marks, in canonical order, which puts the tone mark \emph{before} the quality mark for \vi{ộ ậ ặ ệ} and after the horn for \vi{ớ ứ}); a third, partially composed form inherited from Windows-1258 (quality mark precomposed, tone mark combining) survives in legacy web text. All three are valid and all three occur in the wild. A tokenizer that normalizes its input to NFC erases the difference; one that passes the input through receives a different token sequence for the same word; and some tokenizer implementations \emph{corrupt} combining marks, deleting them outright. Neither of the Gemma tokenizers we audited normalizes (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

\paragraph{Nói lái.}
\vi{Nói lái} is the Vietnamese spoonerism: two syllables of a phrase exchange components so that a new, often humorous or hidden, phrase appears. Writing a syllable as $(O, R, T)$, a variant is a subset of $\{O, R, T\}$ exchanged between the two positions; of the eight subsets, the empty one is the identity and the full one is plain reversal, and the six remaining ones are the six types of the Vietnamese tradition \citep{le-ho-1990-thu-choi-chu,nguyen-van-hiep-noi-lai}: \var{1} swaps the rimes (\vi{mèo cái} $\rightarrow$ \vi{mài kéo}); \var{2} swaps onset and rime together, that is, the syllables trade places while the tones stay in position (\vi{đầu tiên} `first' $\rightarrow$ \vi{tiền đâu} `where's the money?'); \var{3} swaps only the tones (\vi{bí mật} `secret' $\rightarrow$ \vi{bị mất} `got lost'); \var{4} swaps rime and tone, leaving only the onsets in place (\vi{bí mật} $\rightarrow$ \vi{bật mí} `reveal'); \var{5} swaps the onsets and \var{6} onsets and tones. Each variant is an involution, the eight form the group $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^3$, and reading an output in the other syllable order pairs them up: reverse(\var{1}) = \var{6}, reverse(\var{2}) = \var{3}, reverse(\var{4}) = \var{5}. \var{2} and \var{3} therefore produce the same unordered pair (\vi{tiền đâu} and \vi{đâu tiền}), which is why we generate items for the four variants \var{1}--\var{4}, score \task{T1} both in the named order and as an unordered pair, and keep \var{5} and \var{6} in the taxonomy, in the \task{T2} gold and as \task{T3} twins (Section~\ref{sec:tasks}). The literature attaches regional labels to the variants inconsistently: one source calls the tone swap Northern, another calls the whole-syllable swap Northern and the onset-preserving swaps Southern; our variant codes are region-neutral, and validators are region-tagged instead. Nói lái appears in folk sayings, in satire and in the poetry of Hồ Xuân Hương, where it hides meanings a censor would not print; some attested examples have vulgar readings, which our release flags (see the Ethical Considerations section). The only prior computational treatment we know of detects nói lái in classical text with rules and an $n$-gram model \citep{pham-pham-2018-spoonerism}; no study generates, decodes or judges it, and none asks an LLM to.

\paragraph{Character knowledge under subword tokenization.}
A line of work asks what LLMs know about the characters inside their tokens. Subword embeddings encode character identity \citep{kaushal-mahowald-2022-tokens,itzhak-levy-2022-spelling}; CUTE and its multilingual successor EXECUTE test spelling, counting and manipulation \citep{2024-emnlp-main-177,2025-findings-acl-95}, in a set of languages that does not include Vietnamese; PACUTE extends the design to Filipino syllabification and morphology \citep{pacute-2026-filipino}, the closest Southeast Asian analogue, and Phun-Bench tests Chinese homophony, rhyme and tone, finding that models recall pronunciations but cannot use them \citep{phun-bench-2026}. In Chinese and Filipino the phonology is latent behind the script; Vietnamese writes onset, rime and tone in the string, so a failure to manipulate them is attributable to access, not knowledge. \citet{2025-findings-emnlp-719} show that models reconstruct character information in intermediate layers, the template for our probes. \citet{2026-acl-long-634} relate tokenization to phonological knowledge through a syllabification-alignment metric (STAD) defined over English syllabification; our boundary-alignment measure adapts it to boundaries inside a syllable. Tokenizer quality and its cost have been measured across languages \citep{rust-etal-2021-good,petrov-etal-2023-language,ahia-etal-2023-languages}, and the causal effect of tokenization has been estimated by training families of controlled models \citep{2025-acl-long-1374,toksuite-2025}. Re-encodings that leave the code points fixed but change their segmentation lower accuracy across 27 languages (\placeholder{relative drops of 10--24\%, as the sweep recorded them; page-level check of arXiv 2607.26831 pending, Vietnamese coverage unconfirmed}) \citep{ghosh-jyothi-2026-noncanonical}; meaning-preserving re-encodings have been used for Japanese tokenization inconsistency \citep{2025-acl-short-75} and Arabic diacritics, which add information \citep{2026-findings-eacl-22}. For Vietnamese, diacritic removal appears as one of several perturbations in a broad evaluation \citep{2024-findings-naacl-182}, the representation of tone in neural models has been studied for speech \citep{2024-naacl-long-239}, and a grapheme-to-phoneme benchmark for user-generated text has just appeared \citep{ugtphon-2026}. Two recent preprints build new tokenizers, one factorizing Vietnamese syllables into onset, rime and tone before pretraining \citep{2609.21362}, one comparing tokenizers across Southeast Asian languages \citep{2606.15044}; they change the tokenizer, whereas we diagnose deployed ones. Spoonerisms appear in LLM evaluation only as one wordplay type among others in a Russian detection benchmark \citep{kowit24-2025}. The gap, stated exactly: this is the first LLM study of nói lái and the first generative, decoding and validity benchmark of it; no study treats spoonerism as a controlled transformation task; and no study compares canonically equivalent encodings, or tone-placement conventions, of a tonal Latin script on deployed models. Our validity task follows the contrastive design of BLiMP \citep{2020-tacl-1-25} and of the symmetric test sets of \citet{P19-1116}, on which a model that ignores the question scores 50\%.
