\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Written Vietnamese is a syllabic script in a Latin alphabet: every syllable is a separate word whose onset, rime and tone (one of six, marked by a diacritic or its absence) are visible on the page, and Vietnamese has a folk art that takes syllables apart at these seams. \vi{Nói lái} swaps rimes, tones or both between two syllables: \vi{mèo cái} `female cat' becomes \vi{mài kéo} `sharpen scissors', and \vi{bí mật} `secret' becomes \vi{bị mất} `got lost'. The operation works below the word, and the result's spelling depends on its new neighbours (\vi{c} becomes \vi{k} before \vi{eo}).

Large language models do not see this structure: subword tokenizers split syllables where their merge statistics say, and another valid Unicode encoding of the same text changes the split altogether (Figure~\ref{fig:tokens}). Over the 6,595 parsable syllables of the standard spelling list it splits 70\% under NFC, 51.5\% exactly at the onset--rime boundary, and under NFD it spends about one extra token per syllable (1.78 $\rightarrow$ 2.83) and isolates the tone mark as a token of its own in 41.6\% of syllables. We therefore ask: \emph{can a subword LLM reach the onset, rime and tone inside a tonal syllable it never receives as a unit, and does failure grow with how badly the tokens cut across those units?}

Nói lái is our instrument, not our headline. Because its variants permute syllable components and Vietnamese spelling is rule-governed, a rule engine can generate items in any quantity, compute every gold answer and score every output by parsing it, with no LLM judge. The same engine re-encodes text between canonically equivalent Unicode forms and between tone-mark placement conventions with meaning held fixed, a training-free intervention on deployed tokenizers; and the two smallest Gemma~3 models let us ask where tone is represented and whether it is used \citep{2025-findings-emnlp-719}.

We make five contributions.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{\noilai{}}, to our knowledge the first LLM study of nói lái and the first generation, decoding and validity benchmark of it: a rule-verified generator over the four generated variants of a six-type tradition, an attested cultural subset, and a native-validation and human-baseline protocol (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}).
  \item \textbf{A syllable-alignment audit} of how production tokenizers cut onset, rime and tone, related to item-level accuracy across a panel of \placeholder{21} models (Sections~\ref{sec:setup} and~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item \textbf{Orthographic counterfactuals} that change only the token sequence of a tonal Latin script on deployed models, with a census of which tokenizers normalize, pass through or corrupt combining marks (Section~\ref{sec:counterfactuals}).
  \item \textbf{A localization of tone} in small open models with control-validated probes, a structural baseline and activation patching with separate perception and manipulation readouts (Section~\ref{sec:tone}).
  \item \textbf{Reusable tooling}: NFC/NFD re-encoding and diacritic-stripping tools that apply to any diacritic-heavy Latin script; and a Vietnamese syllable parser and spelling-rule engine, released with the generator, which, like the tone-mark placement and partially composed arms, encode Vietnamese phonotactics and spelling and are not script-general.
\end{enumerate}
